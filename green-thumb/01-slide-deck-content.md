## Slide 3: From Crisis to Community — The Birth of NYC Gardens
- Major fiscal crisis in the 1970s, lower-income neighborhoods hit hardest by disinvestment
- Buildings abandoned as residents left the city; vacant lots became dumping grounds and unsafe spaces
- Community gardens formed as acts of resistance, care, and local power
- Bottom-up, community-led spaces (not city-installed)

*Image caption: Liz Christy, founder of Green Guerillas, the first community garden group*

---

## Slide 4: Who is GreenThumb?
**What They Do**
- Operation GreenThumb was launched in 1978
- Since then, expanded and now supports 550+ gardens and 20,000+ volunteers across all five boroughs while preserving open space
- Is the largest community gardening program in the nation

---

## Slide 5: Why Volunteers Matter So Much
- GreenThumb is underfunded (~0.5% of city budget) and relies heavily on volunteer labor
- Volunteers embody the grassroots spirit that created and sustained these gardens — they connect land, community, and everyday care

## Slide 8: System Overview
**GreenThumb operates through a unique partnership model between the city and communities.**
- GreenThumb provides materials, training, and legitimacy to support community gardens across the city
- Each garden operates independently as a neighborhood-driven space
- Volunteers serve as daily stewards, connecting land and community through their care and commitment
- GreenThumb's role is to facilitate and connect — not to own or control these spaces

---

## Slide 9: Unique Perspective
**Independence = Strength**
Gardens control their own priorities, reflect their neighborhoods, and build community on their own terms —
but it also means every garden has different ways of onboarding and engaging volunteers.

## Slide 12: Macro Findings (I)
**Program-Level Constraints**
- GreenThumb supports 550+ gardens with a small team
- Volunteer program run on ~$10,000/year (less than 1% of the city's budget)
- Geographic range covers Staten Island to Far Rockaway — making citywide coordination and outreach difficult
- City bureaucracy slows down how quickly GreenThumb can respond to garden needs

---

## Slide 13: Macro Findings (II)
**How GreenThumb Thinks About Volunteers**
- Before Kyleen, GreenThumb had no citywide volunteer program
- Her team is building a program to bridge community gardeners with everyday New Yorkers, focusing on recruiting committed volunteers, especially younger adults (30s–40s)
- Legacy gardeners from the 1960s–70s are aging out, raising urgent questions about succession and sustaining these spaces
- A lot of volunteers are also seasonal; good, highly skilled people leave after a short period of time

**Quote:** "Our challenge is to keep membership strong, bring in younger generations, and help gardens stay open as older gardeners age out." — Kyleen Sanchez

## Slide 16: Micro Findings — Burnout and Capacity
- Many gardens are entirely volunteer-led, with no paid staff
- A small core group often carries operations, events, admin, and grants
- Leaders described feeling stretched thin, especially when external issues (development, pests, neighborhood change) pile on
- In one garden, membership had to be rebuilt after a leadership fallout, showing how fragile continuity can be
- Across conversations, burnout and succession surfaced as ongoing worries

**Quote:** "Having people get meaningfully engaged is the most important thing because if you can't get a leader off of their position with another one lined up to take up on all of the responsibilities that they've been holding then that burnout can cause like things to just combust." — Abby Highland

---

## Slide 17: Micro Findings — Onboarding and Knowing How to Join
**Gardens lack clear pathways for new volunteers to get involved and stay engaged**
- New volunteers aren't sure what's available, what they're allowed to do, or who to contact
- Clear next steps — how to return, how to get more involved — are often missing or only shared informally
- Without visible pathways, people enjoy a visit but don't see themselves as part of the garden long-term

## Slide 22: From Findings to Next Questions
From our findings, we saw that gardens operate very differently, volunteers struggle with motivation and clear next steps, and a small core group carries most of the work without clear ways to pass on legacy — alongside burnout being tightly linked to a few highly dedicated members holding everything up.

**Comparison — 462 Halsey Farm vs. Oasis Garden**
- *462 Halsey Farm (Top-down Hierarchy):* Bed-Stuy; 30 active members; 100–120 volunteers; first-time "hands in dirt"; farm
- *Oasis Garden (Non-Hierarchical Pedagogy):* Alphabet City (L.E.S.); 30–40 members; difficulty aligning; first-time volunteer tour; native plants; educating about ecosystem
- *Shared:* Rich history; respect for their specific community; strong values; bees & compost; activist-led garden socially and ecologically; debriefing ("circling up") at end of workdays; 100% volunteer-run; engaging participation

**Question:** "How can GreenThumb tackle these issues in a way that's accessible and scalable for all gardens?"

## Slide 24: Section Header
**Insights & Design Opportunities**

---

## Slide 25: Insight #1 — Every Garden Has a Story to Tell
When people don't understand a garden's origins, purpose, or the relationships that shaped it, it becomes harder for them to recognize what they're being asked to protect or contribute to. Without that shared story, newcomers may feel disconnected from the neighborhood context and unsure how their involvement fits into the ongoing narrative. This gap makes it difficult for gardens to build on what already exists — local knowledge, community history, and informal systems that are rarely captured in printed materials. A clear, accessible story helps people see themselves in the garden's past and future, strengthening both belonging and long-term stewardship.

---

## Slide 26: Insight #2 — Early Skill Pathways Grow Roots
When gardens don't have clear ways to surface volunteers' skills and interests early on, it becomes harder to connect people to meaningful contributions over time. Even highly interested volunteers can struggle to see their growth or their role in the larger ecosystem, making it harder for them to stay engaged and rooted in the community. Without this early moment of connection, gardens lose the chance to invite volunteers onto a clear growth path — one that deepens engagement, builds skills, and strengthens long-term participation.

## Slide 28: Prototype Final — Volunteer Pamphlet
**Adaptable take-home guide for first-time volunteers**
- Shares garden's history and community impact
- Connects everyday tasks to the garden's larger mission
- Helps volunteers see their skill growth and personal impact

**Why a pamphlet:**
- Fits into GreenThumb's existing volunteer process
- Tangible, low-barrier, and scalable across 550+ gardens
