# Case Study Content Decisions — Mapping & Rationale

How the case study draws from the slide deck vs. the presenter notes, and why each choice was made. Guiding principles pulled from your portfolio conventions: tight prose over bullets, honest critique over diplomatic hedging, plain confident language, and a clear throughline (here: volunteer continuity, not garden operations broadly).

---

## 1. Opening / "The Tension"

**Used:** Slide stats (550+ gardens, 20,000 volunteers, largest program in the nation, ~0.5% of city budget) combined with the notes-only stat (Jason's "14% of land / 0.5% of budget").

**Why:** The slide version alone makes GreenThumb sound resourced ("largest in the nation"). The notes-only stat reframes that scale as a liability, not an achievement — it's the sharper, more honest opening. Leading with it up front sets the stakes before any findings are introduced, which the deck itself doesn't do until slide 12.

**Left out:** The deck's separate "less than 1%" volunteer-program-specific budget stat (slide 12) — reserved for later, not the open, to avoid stat overload in the first paragraph and because it belongs with the macro findings, not the framing.

---

## 2. Historical Context

**Cut almost entirely.**

**Why:** The 1970s fiscal crisis, Thomas Hoving, the Green Guerillas, and the shift from top-down to inclusive park administration are compelling but not load-bearing for a case study about volunteer onboarding design. Including it would pad the case study with history that doesn't advance the design argument — it's the kind of "diplomatic" scene-setting your portfolio conventions push against. A single line ("bottom-up, community-led, not city-installed") survives in the tension section because it's needed to explain *why* independence is structurally baked into the model.

**Decision rule applied:** if a fact doesn't change a design decision downstream, it's cut, regardless of how good the story is.

---

## 3. Stakeholder Section (Jason & Kyleen)

**Used:** Both direct quotes from the notes (bottom-rung/infrastructure; no portal/no minimum hours), plus the notes-only detail about legacy gardeners aging out and the "passing of the baton" language.

**Why:** These quotes are the sharpest primary-source material in the whole transcript — better than any paraphrase. The deck already surfaces both quotes verbatim, so no invention was needed; the case study just adds the underlying tension notes make explicit (Kyleen balancing *individual garden needs* against *system-wide accessibility*) which the slide bullet-points flatten into a single line.

**Left out:** Kyleen's specific age-bracket target (30s–40s) — interesting for GreenThumb internally, not necessary for a case study about the design artifact.

---

## 4. Macro Findings

**Used:** Small team / large system, geographic spread, bureaucracy — condensed into a single sentence rather than four bullets.

**Why:** Your portfolio note on the insurance documentation work states a clear preference for tight prose over bullet formatting. The deck's bullet list format works fine on a slide; in case-study prose it reads as filler. Converted to a compound sentence that keeps the same information density.

**Decision:** the $10k/year volunteer-program budget stat *is* used here, after being deliberately withheld from the opening (see #1), so it lands as evidence rather than rhetoric.

---

## 5. Garden Leader Interviews (Abby & Catherine)

**Used:** Abby's full quote, and the notes-only detail that burnout/succession "kept coming up... not as hypothetical future problems, but as things people are worried about right now."

**Why:** The deck's Micro Findings slide states the pattern as a bulleted conclusion. The notes show *how the team arrived at the conclusion* — that it surfaced unprompted and repeatedly across separate interviews. That's a research-credibility detail worth keeping: it shows the burnout finding wasn't a hypothesis the team went looking to confirm, it emerged from the data. This matters for a case study audience evaluating research rigor, even though it's invisible on the slide itself.

**Left out:** Catherine Lin doesn't get a distinct quote in the notes (only appears by name/context) — nothing invented or attributed to her that wasn't said.

---

## 6. Fieldwork — Volunteer Day at Green Oasis

**Used:** Nearly all of it, more heavily than any other section, restructured from a timeline into narrative prose focused on function over chronology.

**Why:** This is the one section of the notes with material that never made it to slides at all — the arrival friction point, the specific readings (Kimmerer, Rainer & West), Mackenzie's pandemic-gardening story. It's also the clearest evidence of your own primary research contribution (per the project-portfolio pattern of highlighting your specific authored role rather than the group's undifferentiated output). Per your stated preference for honest critique over hedging, the arrival friction point was kept deliberately rather than smoothed over — it's a real flaw in an otherwise well-run garden, and cutting it for the sake of a clean success story would be dishonest.

**Left out:** The two high-school-student attendees and their specific behavior — mentioned in notes, adds color but no design signal. Cut per the "does this change a downstream decision" rule.

**Reframed:** deck slide 19 presents this as a pure timeline; case study leads instead with the notes' own framing sentence ("we observed where comfort and confusion show up... provided a map of where interventions could sit") because that sentence *is* the bridge between fieldwork and insight — the timeline alone doesn't argue anything.

---

## 7. Survey

**Used:** Both findings, plus the explicit low-response-rate caveat (3 of 20 gardens) stated as a limitation rather than smoothed into a confident claim.

**Why:** Directly follows your stated preference for honest critique. The deck presents the 3-garden number neutrally, almost as a footnote ("we got responses from 3 gardens"). The case study makes the limitation explicit and names it as directional, not conclusive — this is a deliberate editorial choice to not let a small sample dress up as validated research.

---

## 8. Insights → Prototype Convergence

**Used:** The notes-only detail that the team "decided to focus on Insight 2... but then discovered it went beyond that" into emotional/historical connection.

**Why:** This is the single most important sentence in the notes for case-study purposes and it isn't on any slide. The deck presents Insight 1 and Insight 2 as parallel, separate findings (slides 25–26) and then jumps straight to the pamphlet with no visible seam. The notes reveal the actual design reasoning — that the two insights merged into one artifact — which is exactly the kind of process transparency a case study should show and a slide deck, built for a live audience, doesn't need to.

**Left out:** The three-way prototype comparison table (pamphlet vs. community web visualization vs. workshop onboarding design) from slide 27 was *not* expanded into full case-study prose — summarized instead as "we considered other formats" implicitly through the "why a pamphlet" framing, since walking through three abandoned concepts in detail would dilute focus from the one that shipped. This is a case-study-length/pacing decision, not a factual omission — worth revisiting if you want a "roads not taken" section.

---

## 9. Prototype Description

**Used:** Deck content almost verbatim (history + task breakdown + reflection page) since this is where slide and notes agree closely and the deck's language is already tight.

**Why:** No meaningful gap between sources here — the slide already reads like it was drafted from the notes directly. Minimal editorializing applied.

---

## 10. Limits / Honesty Section

**Added, not sourced from either deck or notes directly** — this is editorial synthesis, built from combining: the "how does leadership use this without added burden" open question (notes, Next Steps) + the untested-prototype status (implied throughout, never stated as a limitation in either source) + the survey response-rate caveat (#7 above).

**Why:** Neither the deck nor the notes explicitly frame these as *limitations* — they're phrased as forward-looking "next steps," which is appropriate for a live pitch but reads as unexamined confidence in a written case study. Per your preference for honest critique over diplomatic hedging, this section was added deliberately as its own beat rather than folded into "Next Steps," so the limitation reads as an admission, not a segue.

---

## 11. Next Steps

**Used:** Deck's five-part structure, condensed into three sentences of prose, restoring the two ideas from the notes that give it more edge than the slide's bullet phrasing: the pamphlet as a possible advocacy tool (not just onboarding), and testing specifically at 462 Halsey and Green Oasis by name (the deck genericizes this to "workdays").

**Why:** Naming the specific gardens ties Next Steps back to the actual relationships built during research, rather than leaving it abstract — consistent with keeping the throughline concrete rather than templated.

---

## What Was Deliberately Excluded Throughout

- Historical/political backstory (Hoving, fiscal crisis specifics) — doesn't serve the design argument.
- Full three-concept prototype comparison — pacing decision, flagged above as revisitable.
- Minor fieldwork color (high school students) — no design signal.
- Kyleen's specific recruitment demographic target — internal GreenThumb detail, not case-study-relevant.

**General rule used throughout:** if a detail exists only in the notes and changes how a reader understands *why* a design decision was made, it was pulled in even though it's absent from the slides. If a detail exists on the slides but is purely presentational (bullet formatting, stat repetition, section-header slides), it was compressed or cut in favor of tighter prose.
