# Field Notes — Volunteer Day at Green Oasis Community Garden

**Observer:** Philip Cote
**Location:** Green Oasis Community Garden, Lower East Side
**Participants:** Surya, Catherine (garden leaders), two high school students (volunteering for credit), Philip

---

## Arrival

Walked up to the garden and found a group of 4–5 volunteers already working, pulling invasive species from a bed. Approached them and explained my business; they directed me to the back of the garden, where I met Surya and Catherine.

## Setup

Surya and Catherine launched the tour from a picnic table already loaded with the tools of ongoing garden life: pens, notebooks, nature books, post-its, coffee cups, book bags. Not a staged workshop setup — the table doubled as both meeting point and evidence of how the space is actually used day to day.

## Workshop Structure (Storyline)

1. **Tour** — provides context to the garden for newcomers, sets the stage for the workshop, gives the group a chance to get to know each other and build rapport. History told first, before anything else.
2. **Nature journaling** — given a worksheet and notebook, encouraged to explore the garden and record observations based on three prompts:
   - What do you notice?
   - What do you wonder?
   - What does it remind you of?
   - (Framed around two modes of thinking: science as empirical, math as deductive.)
   - Focus on relationships — e.g. evidence of human activity, the gazebo and the ivy growing on it.
   - Sequence: start with observations and journaling, then build a relationship web.
3. **Discussion** — group shares journaling observations.
4. **Ecosystem mapping** — collective exercise illustrating interconnectedness within the garden.
5. **Reading** — two excerpts shared aloud.
6. **Discussion** — closing conversation over tea.

## Field Notes — Garden Operations & Constraints

- No food-growing in shaded areas; trees make parts of the garden too shady for it.
- Composting: members contribute food scraps, but limited to members only.
- Common misconception: many things labeled "compostable" (paper products, etc.) usually aren't, in practice.
- Mulch is actively maintained — protects soil from carbon leaching due to sun exposure, and helps retain nutrients in the soil.
- Open question raised by the leaders themselves: how self-sufficient can a garden be, where everything stays fertile on its own?
- Raised beds have been in place since the garden's founding in the 1980s; used to grow vegetables, owned by individual members.
- The garden was originally formed from the merger of two separate gardens.
- Wooden sculptures on site need to be brought in for winterizing each year — an example of internal maintenance the garden community has to manage on its own.
- Unpermitted dumping is an ongoing issue — the garden faces misuse problems common to public spaces generally.
- Parks (the city agency) can lag on things like site updates.

## Field Notes — Ecology

- The garden is framed internally as an ecosystem, explicitly compared to a forest.
- Native plants support bee populations; the garden maintains a dedicated native plant plot.
- Plants are understood in combination, not isolation — how they relate to each other (e.g. ground cover) matters as much as any individual plant.
- Each plant is described as occupying its own niche.
- Root systems vary meaningfully by plant — some are shallow and spread outward, others grow deep and downward.
- The pond was dug by hand by the garden's original founders — holds approximately 20,000 gallons, stocked with koi, and develops a mosquito problem in summer.
- Beehives are maintained on site; wild bees are noted as having distinct relationships to the space compared to the managed hives.

## Field Notes — Community & Identity

- The garden is also used as a theatre/performance space — hosts free events, including visits from an opera company.
- Art is intended to interact with the garden, not just sit in it.
- Surya and Catherine expressed interest in building a more comprehensive oral history project for the garden.
- Maintaining the garden's aesthetic is treated as its own explicit priority, alongside ecological and community functions.

## Field Notes — Membership & Open Questions

- Unclear/unresolved during the visit: exact membership count, range of member activity levels, and how membership payment works.
- Estimated 35–40 members "drift off" (disengage over time) — exact retention is hard to quantify.
- A large share of the volunteer base is high school students.
- Some volunteers do return over time, but again — difficult to quantify with any precision.
- Surya and Catherine noted there's an opportunity for the garden to be structured differently than it currently is.
