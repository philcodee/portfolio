## Historical Context

NYC faced a major fiscal crisis that led to widespread cuts in city services. Lower-income neighborhoods were hit hardest — buildings were abandoned as residents fled the city, and vacant lots became dumping grounds and unsafe spaces. But communities survived. Neighbors rallied together, adopting these abandoned lots and converting them into green spaces. These gardens emerged as acts of resistance, care, and local power — bottom-up, community-led spaces born from people demanding something better for their neighborhoods.

What was once run by the community eventually evolved into a city-run program. Today, even with the support of city-run programs, gardens still face challenges — impending real estate development, construction disturbances. NYC gardens are a symbol of community support, a center of community.

Thomas Hoving's inclusive style and the "vest pocket park" campaign sought to quickly transform vacant lots into usable open space, often soliciting help and advice from local residents — this helped change the tone of park administration during this era. This shift, coupled with the severe fiscal crisis of the early 1970s, paved the way for the Community Garden movement of the 1970s, which began in earnest on the Lower East Side with the Green Guerillas. Thanks to both the shift in administrative styles from top-down to inclusive, and local residents building on these lots from the bottom-up, the tone was set for how community gardens continue to be supported by the city and its residents today. What started as a grassroots movement eventually evolved into a city-run program, and that collaborative spirit — where the city facilitates but communities lead — still defines how these gardens operate today.

Operation GreenThumb was launched in 1978 to support this movement, and critically, it gave community gardens legitimate representation. Since then, it has expanded to support over 550 gardens and 20,000 volunteers across all five boroughs. It's now the largest community gardening program in the nation, providing materials, training, and that essential legal voice for these spaces.

## Volunteers

Volunteers embody the grassroots spirit that created and sustained these gardens. They're not just tending plants — they connect land, community, and everyday care. Volunteers are vital for the community, providing spaces for neighbors to gather, learn, and build relationships. And critically, volunteers have been key to preservation: when garden plots face threats from development, it's volunteers who organize, protest, and fight to protect these spaces.

## System Overview

GreenThumb helps community gardens create green spaces and strengthen neighborhoods — but how do they actually work behind the scenes? GreenThumb operates through a unique partnership model. The city provides materials, training, and legitimacy, but each garden operates independently as a neighborhood-driven space. Each individual garden has its own approach to plot ownership, events, onboarding, etc. Volunteers serve as daily stewards, connecting land and community through their care. GreenThumb facilitates and connects — it doesn't own or control these spaces.

This independence is both a strength and a challenge. Gardens control their own priorities and reflect their neighborhoods, but every garden has different ways of onboarding and engaging volunteers. We see it as a strength, but there are unique challenges that come with it.

## GreenThumb Staff Interviews

This is Jason and Kyleen. Jason is the Assistant Director of Operations at GreenThumb, and Kyleen is a Volunteer Program Coordinator. Both have extensive backgrounds in volunteer work, though Kyleen's role focuses more specifically on working with individual gardens. Both have a deep passion for the role that GreenThumb plays in the city.

As Assistant Director of Operations, Jason sees a problem in the general public perception of volunteering as "bottom-rung." When in reality, it is the lifeblood which allows for enrichment and nourishment to diverse communities and public lands. He also sees designers as a conduit into helping advocate for change in this public perception.

In her role as Volunteer Program Coordinator, Kyleen's primary challenge is to recruit for a diverse range of gardens — especially considering that their point of entry is meant to be accessible for all. She needs to strike a balance between meeting the needs of the individual garden as well as the needs of an array of skill sets, interest levels, and intent to engage (either once or multiple times).

Beyond this, what are the issues they face as an organization?
- **Lack of resources:** GreenThumb is overextended.
- **Small team managing a large system:** A big scope of gardens run by a small team of operations and volunteer coordinators (agents for the garden).
- **Limited budget:** As Jason told us, "Parks manages about 14% of New York City's land with just 0.5% of the city's budget."
- **Every garden is different:** Whether it's a farm whose beds are in danger of being damaged by construction debris, or simply not knowing the difference between native plants and invasive species, Kyleen needs to make sure that every garden is set up for success.

Despite all these challenges, they've managed to largely standardize their practices and support this vast network. With the citywide volunteer program being relatively new, Kyleen's main focus is on recruitment, especially with younger adults. To compound that challenge, legacy gardeners are now starting to age out, creating a gap and need for a "passing of the baton" — the transfer of valuable generational knowledge about a specific garden's history and practices.

Kyleen: "Our challenge is to keep membership strong, bring in younger generations, and help gardens stay open as older gardeners age out."

## Garden Leader Interviews

In order to dive deeper into GreenThumb's macro problems, we also wanted to hear from some garden leaders. After speaking with GreenThumb employees Jason and Kyleen, we had the opportunity to interview Abby Highland, garden leader at 462 Halsey Community Garden, and Catherine Lin, a garden leader at Green Oasis Community Garden.

There was something that came up repeatedly in our interviews: burnout and capacity. As we kept talking to garden leaders, we started noticing this pattern underneath everything else. These gardens are entirely volunteer-led, with no paid staff — it's all community members doing this in their free time. What ends up happening is a small core group does everything: managing operations, planning events, handling admin work, all of it. And the leaders we talked to kept describing burnout — not just from the day-to-day garden work, but from external pressures too: development threats, pest infestations, neighborhood changes. When those things pile on top of everything else, it becomes overwhelming.

Abby talked about how her garden had to completely rebuild their membership after a leadership fallout. That shows how fragile the structure can be — these spaces depend entirely on a few people staying engaged and getting along, and if that breaks down, the whole garden is at risk.

Across all our conversations, burnout and succession kept coming up — not as hypothetical future problems, but as things people are worried about right now. The current leaders are exhausted, and there's no clear path for who takes over next. That's the reality of the volunteer retention challenge: the people keeping these gardens alive are exhausted, without a sustainable way forward.

Another thing we found: because these organizations are volunteer-led, the onboarding process ends up being unclear. New volunteers aren't sure what's available to them, what they're allowed to do, or who to contact if they want to get more involved. The bigger issue is that clear next steps are often missing — after someone finishes their first workday, they don't know how to come back or take on more responsibility. Sometimes this gets shared informally, but it's not consistent. So people can have a great experience and enjoy the workday, but they don't see themselves as part of the garden long-term. There's no visible pathway showing them how to become a regular volunteer or eventually a member.

Garden leaders aren't intentionally keeping this information hidden. When you're volunteer-led and already stretched thin, formalizing these pathways just falls to the bottom of the list. But without them, even interested volunteers don't know how to stay connected beyond that first visit.

Abby: "Having people get meaningfully engaged is the most important thing, because if you can't get a leader off of their position with another one lined up to take up on all of the responsibilities that they've been holding, then that burnout can cause things to just combust."

## Survey

To further expand our understanding of volunteer experiences for community gardens, we sent out a survey. We got responses from 3 of the 20 gardens we reached out to. Two findings shared across the 3 gardens:
1. **Discoverability by entry-level volunteers:** Referrals from members and walk-ins during public hours were how volunteers most often find their garden.
2. **Willingness to return:** Feeling welcome, having clear instructions, and hands-on learning were the aspects that respondents felt made volunteers more likely to return.

## Key Findings and Themes

From our findings, we're seeing a pattern: gardens all operate differently, volunteers struggle with motivation and clarity on how to stay involved, and a small core group is carrying most of the work without clear ways to pass on knowledge or responsibility. These tensions point beyond any single garden and raise a bigger challenge for the system as a whole: how can GreenThumb tackle these issues in a way that's accessible and scalable for all gardens?

We decided to focus on Insight 2 because we wanted to talk about specific skill sets — but then discovered it went beyond that, allowing volunteers to connect to the garden emotionally by learning its history, seeing their progress, seeing clear standardized expectations, and understanding how they impact the gardens.

## Insight 1: Every Garden Has a Story to Tell

One of our key insights is that every garden has a story to tell. Without understanding origins, people can't recognize what they're being asked to protect. This isn't just about conservation language — it's about neighborhood-focused narratives that help volunteers connect to the place and its history. When volunteers understand how their garden came to be — the neighbors who fought for it, the community needs it addresses — they're more invested in its future. The challenge is building on what's already in place and finding ways to share these stories that go beyond traditional printed materials.

This leads us to ask: How might we accommodate all levels of engagement for garden-curious newcomers? How do we meet people where they are and help them see themselves as part of the garden's ongoing story?

## Insight 2: Early Skill Pathways Grow Roots

Our second insight focuses on skill development and volunteer autonomy. While repeat volunteers show their interest by returning, gardens struggle to capture and develop their specific skill sets due to unclear entry points in the onboarding process. What we heard from garden leaders is the need for transparency — volunteers need to clearly see how they can connect with the garden. But they also need tools to find answers on their own, to become resourceful without depending on a garden member. This goes beyond just doing tasks — it's about empowering volunteers to grow independently.

**How Might We:** Ensure that volunteers can quickly see the value their skill set would have on the garden over time?

## Prototype Development

Inspired by our insights, our time at Green Oasis Community Garden, and the GreenThumb handbook, we chose a simple pamphlet — a series that breaks down activities volunteers might be involved in. After the garden leader introduces the space and shares the garden's history, the pamphlet is handed out before everyone gets started.

The pamphlet helps volunteers understand their role. It connects everyday tasks — spreading soil, digging, planting, watering — to how those actions support the garden's larger mission. Having this baseline understanding helps volunteers feel more confident in what they're doing, more connected to the work and the community as a whole, adding to a sense of belonging.

On the inside panel, it starts with the message "You Make the Garden Thrive." This section frames volunteers as part of a larger legacy — showing how their tasks help the garden flourish, support wildlife, and contribute to community pride. It gives a quick overview of what to expect while gardening, the tools they'll use, and how their work makes a real, lasting difference. Below that is the "About GreenThumb" section, covering their history and what they do — giving volunteers context for the broader movement they're contributing to. On the back side, there's space for reflection and notes — volunteers can write or draw about what they're learning, track their skills, or capture their personal experience. This reinforces the idea that volunteering isn't just about tasks — it's about connecting to the garden, the community, and their own growth.

Inside, the "What Can You Expect While Gardening?" section breaks down tasks in detail — how they're done, what tools are used, what they look like, and how they benefit the garden. It gives volunteers a clear understanding of what they'll be doing that day, helping them feel confident, connected, and purposeful. We envision using hand-drawn, custom illustrations to give the section a warm, hands-on feel that reflects the gardening experience.

## Next Steps

Moving forward, we'd love to test this prototype with actual volunteers and garden leaders. Ideally, we'd figure out how to capture the information volunteers write down. Garden leaders could use this for planning workdays, understanding what resonates with people, or identifying who might want to return. We'd need to determine what's most useful to collect and how leadership can actually use it without creating more work for themselves.

For volunteers, the pamphlet becomes their starting point — something they can reference between visits, track their progress with, and use to decide if they want to get more involved. Eventually, we'd bring it back to GreenThumb for feedback. We want to know if they see this as practical to roll out across their network, and whether they see it as more than just onboarding — if it could work as a form of advocacy, helping volunteers understand the stakes and become advocates for community gardens beyond their first workday. The goal would be to refine this based on real use and make sure it serves both volunteers and the gardens.
