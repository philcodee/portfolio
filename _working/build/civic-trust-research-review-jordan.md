# Review Thread — Civic Trust Research

**Jordan Voss** · Design Director

**Read time estimate.** This is a research write-up, not a case study — seven sections, four research themes, five lifecycle stages, an Arnstein's Ladder detour, three problem statements, and seven design principles. At my reading speed that's 10+ minutes of real content. I have four. I will not finish this, and I will not feel bad about it — that's the page's problem to solve, not mine.

**First thing I noticed.** The hero does its job: "Building Civic Trust," then immediately two big numbers — 42% and 13% — stacked in their own blocks. That's the right instinct. Numbers first, framing second. I'm oriented in under five seconds, and the outcomes grid right below (17% / 5 / 7) reinforces it. Good landing. If the rest of the page matched this density-to-insight ratio, I'd stay.

**Where I'd drop off.** Right at "Research Synthesis." Four side-by-side blocks — Cognitive Load, Lack of Process Maps, Stigma & Fear, Time Poverty — each a dense paragraph that *describes* the theme rather than landing a finding. They all read the same rhythm: abstract noun phrase, then a paragraph explaining what it means. Nothing in any of those four blocks gives me a number, a quote, or a moment to grab onto — and that's exactly the section where the page asks me to absorb the most. That's where I scroll past without reading a word, and once I've done that once, I do it for every subsequent "wall of two-column entries" — which is most of the rest of the page.

**Copy to cut or tighten.**
- "The Data," "Problems, Causes & Opportunities," "Design Principles" — these are filing-cabinet labels. They tell me what *kind* of content is coming, not what it says. Compare to your own hero, which leads with "42%" instead of a header that says "Statistics." Hold the rest of the page to that bar.
- The three "How might we…" prompts in Problems all open identically and run long — by the third one I'm pattern-matching, not reading. Pick the sharpest one and cut the other two, or compress all three into a single framing statement with the distinct angles called out in bold.
- Paragraph ledes like "Administrative complexity creates barriers experienced not as functional difficulty but as an emotional ordeal…" — that's a thesis statement wearing a topic-sentence costume. Lead with the conclusion ("Complexity isn't a usability problem — it's an emotional one") and let the explanation follow for whoever keeps reading.
- The Civic Lifecycle diagram is the most interesting structural idea on the page (five stages, one highlighted as where CivicGuide intervenes) but it's introduced with a caption ("Core insight threading all stages: consistent infrastructure creates...") that buries the actual insight — that Stage 3 is where the whole project lives — in the *fourth* line of an item inside the diagram ("← CivicGuide targets this"). That should be the headline of the section, not an aside.

**One move that would change it.** Pull one hard number or sharp quote out of *every* major section and stage it the way you staged 42%/13% in the hero — as a standalone callout, not a sentence buried in paragraph three. You already have the ammunition: "8% of Black Americans trusted government in 2014," "the Low-Income archetype experiences every other group's barriers simultaneously," "Stage 3 is where CivicGuide intervenes." Right now those are the strongest things on the page and they're the hardest things to find. Surface them, and a four-minute scan would actually cover the spine of your research. Leave them buried, and the spine is invisible to anyone who isn't already convinced to read closely — which, in a hiring review, is no one.
