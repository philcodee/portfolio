# Building Civic Trust — Findings Summary
**Course:** Framing User Experiences II  
**Focus:** Reducing intimidation & overwhelm by helping people understand how to engage with local civic resources

---

## Design Brief

### Goal
Improve how people understand, navigate, and trust public systems and institutions, by helping them engage with local civic resources without feeling intimidated or overwhelmed.

### Problem Framing: Isolated Hurdles
Civic resources are rarely experienced as a single coherent system. Seen through a lifecycle lens, they appear as a shifting set of touchpoints that change with age, context, and circumstance. As a result, civic resources function less like supportive infrastructure and more like a series of isolated hurdles: forms to complete, meetings to discover, portals to decipher, deadlines to remember. The issue is not motivation, but **legibility, timing, and coordination**.

### Civic Resources vs. Civic Engagement (Distinction)
- **Civic Resources** — used on an individual or group level, in an infrastructural context, of a transactional or administrative nature (e.g., NYC 311)
- **Civic Engagement** — takes place on the community level, is social, participatory, and agentic in nature (e.g., voting, city planning)

---

## Synthesis: Research Themes

High-level themes were identified across both civic resources and civic engagement. Central to the synthesis is the correlation between the two categories and how civic trust is repaired or damaged as a result of a lack or abundance in either.

### Cognitive Load
Administrative complexity creates barriers to engagement, experienced not merely as functional difficulty but as an emotional ordeal — often discouraging participation.

### Lack of Process Maps
Informational opacity causes overwhelm even in motivated individuals. They know a problem exists but cannot decipher the process to solve it.

### Stigma & Fear
Psychological costs appear via intimidation with state interactions, particularly among those who feel vulnerable or unwelcome because of their identity. Immigrant and undocumented communities, specifically, worry that seeking help will put them at risk of detention or deportation.

### Time Poverty
All barriers tend to affect those with the least amount of time or resources — a key mechanism in maintaining power asymmetries.

### Supporting Frameworks Referenced
- **Arnstein's Ladder of Citizen Participation** — non-participatory or tokenized people are most affected by civic systems; the ladder describes degrees of citizen power from manipulation to citizen control
- **The Medicare Maze** — a prime example of a process which should be accessible to all but is intentionally opaque
- **Administrative Burden** — when hassles are not well targeted, they often end up affecting those who most need help

## Data Visualization: Civic Trust Trends (1970–2020)

### Core Thesis
Consistent delivery of civic resources & services can lead to greater trust in government, which in turn can contribute to a higher probability of an increase in civic engagement.

**Chain:** Civic Resources → Trust → Civic Engagement

### Orlando Case Study
Research focused on how a rapidly growing city can build relationships with new residents and promote active citizenship through the effective delivery of digital city services. Key themes identified: the intersection of technology, service delivery, and civic trust.

**Trust as Effective Service Delivery:** Trust is forged through the effective execution of "mundane" services like trash collection, road maintenance, and water delivery.

### Civic Trust in America (Pew Research / GSS / ANES, 1958–2020)
- **2010 snapshot:** The financial crisis erased recovery. Low-income trust fell to 13%. Young adults — most optimistic in 1972 — dropped to 24%.
- National trust average: 19%
- Breakdown by demographic at the 2010 low: White 22%, Black 14%, Hispanic 17%, Low-Income 13%, Young Adults 24%
- Resource deficit areas measured: Healthcare 16%, Broadband 35%, Civic Voice 28%, Housing 35%, Food Security 15%

## User Mapping: Barrier Patterns by Group

### Age
- Young adults face intimidation and procedural confusion
- Older adults are often locked out of digital-first services

### Region
- Rural communities suffer from sparse infrastructure
- Urban residents face bureaucratic overload

### Cultural Context
- Minority communities face compounding distrust from historical exclusion
- Immigrant communities are excluded by language and process complexity

### Socio-Economic Status
- Low-income households face every barrier at once: no time, eroded trust, civic skill gaps, power asymmetry, and often a history of exclusion

| Archetype | Description |
|---|---|
| **Expressive Youth** | Young adults increasingly active in protests, social media |
| **Dense Urban** | High resource density, policy comment activity |

| **Low-Income** | Survival mode; tokenized — consulted, rarely empowered |

## User Mapping: Civic Lifecycle

Core insight threading all stages: **consistent infrastructure creates the conditions for deeper participation.**

### Stage 1 — Exposure
- **Context:** Infrastructure experienced indirectly through schools, parks, transit, libraries
- **Feelings:** Comfort, neutrality, background trust
- **Social dynamics:** Family, neighborhood, social communities
- **Nature of experience:** Passive, yet formative — shaping future attitudes
- **Power structure:** Invisible; institutions in the background
- **Cultural dimension:** Resource quality varies by neighborhood

### Stage 2 — Awareness
- **Context:** Civic systems encountered through voting, or searching for housing, jobs, school
- **Feelings:** Curiosity, confusion, intimidation
- **Social dynamics:** Family, peers, schools, media
- **Capabilities:** Register to vote, attend meetings, leave comments
- **Power structure:** Hierarchies become visible; bureaucracy is perceived
- **Cultural dimension:** Civic knowledge sharing varies by community

### Stage 3 — Transaction
- **Context:** Practical problems solved by acquiring permits, paying taxes, applying for benefits, enrolling in higher education
- **Feelings:** Frustration, anxiety, relief after completion
- **Social dynamics:** Informal networks, neighborly support, community organizations
- **Behavior:** Navigate, seek help, abandon tasks, give up
- **Power structure:** Power asymmetries visible; institutions control the process
- **Cultural dimension:** Marginalized groups carry higher administrative burden

### Stage 4 — Participation
- **Context:** Civic decisions influenced through public meetings, participatory budgeting, community advocacy
- **Feelings:** Empowerment, anger, solidarity
- **Social dynamics:** Collective action; stronger ties between interest groups
- **Capabilities:** Organized advocacy; engagement with decision-making process
- **Power structure:** Arnstein's ladder becomes visible — degrees of tokenism vs. genuine power-sharing
- **Cultural dimension:** Historical trust shapes voice; past exclusions inhibit future participation

### Stage 5 — Influence
- **Context:** Citizens shape policy & systems through community boards, elected office, planning commissions
- **Feelings:** Pride, responsibility, long-term commitment
- **Social dynamics:** Networking with policy makers, civil servants, advocacy orgs
- **Capabilities:** Allocate resources; respond to community input
- **Power structure:** Top of Arnstein's ladder; citizen leaders wield formal power
- **Cultural dimension:** Representation gaps persist; access shaped by race & class

## Problems: Causes & Consequences

The core problem framing — civic resources as isolated hurdles — is always coupled with **compounding systemic barriers**.

### Lack of Civic Influence
Where a person lands on the spectrum of influence in the civic landscape will cause similar degrees of intimidation or overwhelm, felt exponentially by those who have the least influence on policy and collective mindset.
- **Supporting image concept:** Participation-for-profit — pseudo-participatory programs attempt to convince citizens that they are the problem

### Administrative Burden
Transactional experiences are tedious, opaque, and confusing. This administrative burden is felt most by those who have not been provided sufficient time, information, or education to successfully navigate the systemic maze.
- **Supporting framework:** The Gulfs of Evaluation & Execution (Norman / NN/g) — the user must execute, evaluate the outcome, plan the next step, evaluate that outcome, and so on — an amplified loop in civic systems

### Simultaneous Barriers
Experienced during unexpected or high-stress moments and rarely seen in isolation. To someone who is already time scarce and fears civic interaction because of their identity, these barriers are dehumanizing.
- **Supporting image concept:** Neighbors in danger — those who can, stand up for one another toward more fair and humane treatment for all

## Opportunities: How Might We Questions

### HMW 1 — Empowering Agency & Influence
*Stemming from: Lack of Civic Influence*  
How might we **empower people with agency and influence** when engaging with local civic resources?

### HMW 2 — Systemic Navigation Assistance
*Stemming from: Administrative Burden*  
How might we **provide meaningful assistance navigating systemic barriers** when engaging with local civic resources?

### HMW 3 — Fair & Humane Treatment
*Stemming from: Simultaneous Barriers*  
How might we **ensure the fair and humane treatment of a person's time & identity** when engaging with local civic resources?

---

## Hypotheses

### Hypothesis 1 — Empowering with Agency & Influence
We believe that if we visualize the immediate positive effects that civic participation can have on the quality and timeliness of civic resources for non-participatory or historically tokenized citizens or peoples, then we will see an impact on policies concerning the areas requiring the greatest improvements as determined by those citizens.

**Measure:** Upticks in civic trust — efficacy of feedback loop through public trust surveys and qualitative data collection.

### Hypothesis 2 — Systemic Navigation Assistance
We believe that if we help to create novel pathways through well-known systemic barriers for non-participatory or historically tokenized citizens or peoples, then we will see a tangible shift in quality of life for all.

**Measure:** Decrease in systemic-born issues — effectiveness of assistance through monitoring preventable, known system barriers.

### Hypothesis 3 — Fair & Humane Treatment
We believe that if we develop a framework of humane ethics for human-run and machine-run systems which provide civic resources for the benefit of non-participatory or historically tokenized citizens or peoples, then we will see more civic participation with the goal of calling for adjustments to the delivery of civic resources.

**Measure:** Increase in effective resource delivery — effectiveness of delivery measured with timely quality assurance surveys and open source policy voting.

## Design Principles: Civic Experience Framework

Seven principles to guide decision-making when designing products or services for civic users.

1. **Legibility of Civic Systems** — Civic systems should be legible to ordinary residents without requiring specialized civic knowledge or institutional familiarity.

2. **Predictability and Consistency** — Civic resources should behave consistently across time, institutions, and geographic contexts.

3. **Cognitive Accessibility** — Civic systems should minimize unnecessary cognitive load and allow people to navigate them without extensive interpretation.

4. **Distributed Civic Knowledge** — Civic knowledge should be embedded in environments and systems rather than requiring individuals to possess extensive prior knowledge.

5. **Equitable Access to Civic Infrastructure** — Civic resources should be distributed in ways that do not systematically disadvantage particular communities.

6. **Meaningful Participation** — Opportunities for civic engagement should have clear pathways to influence outcomes.

7. **Institutional Transparency** — Civic systems should make the relationship between actions and outcomes visible.
