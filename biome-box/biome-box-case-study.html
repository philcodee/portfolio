The Problem

Finding engaging, screen-free play is harder than it sounds.

As a parent, I wanted to stay genuinely present when playing with Henry — not just supervising. Most alternatives to screens either weren't engaging enough for me or weren't engaging enough for him.

That sent me looking for a framework to define what "good" interactive play actually looks like. The E-AIMS model gave me a clear design directive: mirror the real world. That became my north star.

"Content is engaging, kids are actively involved, it feels meaningful — and it's social. So ideally, you or another family member is watching it with them."

— NPR, on the E-AIMS model for interactive media

Research + Discovery

What kids respond to — and what I already knew from the floor.

I looked at what was already in our house: a Yoto Player, a Terra Busy Board. Research on learning environments pointed to tactile, exploratory materials that draw children's attention through physical interaction.

I'd also spent hours playing with Henry — enough to know that novelty wears off fast and physical interaction holds his attention longer than passive content.

That pointed me toward sensors and a microprocessor. I landed on a joystick and piezos as the core input system and started experimenting with form.

How It Works

Tap a creature. Move it through its world.

Each of the 4 piezo contacts maps to a creature card in the left sidebar. Tap a contact and that creature's sound starts looping — and its sprite becomes the cursor on screen.

The joystick moves that creature around the biome landscape with eased, drifting movement — like guiding an animal through its environment. Press the joystick button and the scene shifts to the next biome, sounds stop, and you start fresh.

The four colored circles on the physical device match the four colored circles on the UI cards — green, blue, purple, red. That one-to-one mapping is what Henry locked onto: he could look at the box and know which tap would summon which animal.

Key Feedback + Learnings

What the critiques surfaced.

01

Tactility was the hook

Users found the physical form factor engaging and comfortable. Keeping input physical — not screen-mediated — was the right call.

02

Controls were opaque

No one knew what the joystick did without trying it. Mapping needed to be more intuitive — or more legible in the UI.

03

E-AIMS came in at midterm

The paper prototype didn't yet follow the research framework. The shift to biome-based UI was how analogous, real-world relationships came back in.

04

Engagement has a half-life

Henry recognized the circles on the device matched the screen — a win. But he lost interest quickly, pointing to a depth problem the next version would need to solve.

Outcomes

Tactile first. Screen optional.

The final Biome Box used 4 contact mics glued in a square pattern, an integrated joystick, and a foam core enclosure with laminated colored paper. The UI was rebuilt around biome imagery — giving Henry a real-world analogy to hold onto.

When he recognized the circles on the device matched the screen, something clicked. That moment — a physical thing mapping to something on screen — is what the E-AIMS framework asks for.

Next: Solve the depth problem. Novelty fades fast — the next version needs loops, progression, or surprise to hold attention past the first session.
