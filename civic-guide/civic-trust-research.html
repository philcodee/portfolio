The Design Brief

Goal

Improve how people understand, navigate, and trust public institutions — by helping them engage with civic resources without feeling intimidated or overwhelmed.

Problem Framing

Civic resources function less like supportive infrastructure and more like a series of isolated hurdles: forms to complete, meetings to discover, portals to decipher, deadlines to remember. The issue is not motivation — it is legibility, timing, and coordination.

Research Synthesis

All four themes fall hardest on people who already have the least capacity to absorb them — and they compound.

Cognitive Load

Administrative complexity creates barriers experienced not as functional difficulty but as an emotional ordeal — confusing, high-stakes, and unforgiving of wrong turns.

Lack of Process Maps

Informational opacity causes overwhelm even in motivated individuals — they know the problem but cannot decipher the process to solve it. A system that requires institutional knowledge to navigate is exclusionary by design.

Stigma & Fear

For immigrant and undocumented residents, seeking civic assistance can expose them to detention or deportation — the help itself becomes the threat.

Time Poverty

Every barrier lands hardest on those with the fewest resources to absorb it. That's not incidental — it is the mechanism by which power asymmetries in civic systems are maintained.

Trust has been falling for 50 years — and falling unevenly

Trust has declined almost continuously since 1972 — but the decline has not been equal, and the gap between groups has never closed.

User Mapping

Who carries the weight?

Barriers cluster along four axes — age, region, cultural context, and income — and the overlap is where the experience becomes dehumanizing.

  • Young adults: procedural confusion; systems feel designed for someone else
  • Older adults: locked out of digital-first services
  • Rural: sparse infrastructure, long travel to service points
  • Minority communities: compounding distrust from historical exclusion
  • Immigrant communities: excluded by language, complexity, and fear
  • Low-income households: every barrier at once

Seven principles, each closing one gap

1 — Legibility of Civic Systems

Legible to ordinary residents without specialized knowledge — navigation shouldn't require a guide.

2 — Predictability and Consistency

Behaves the same across time, institutions, and geography. Inconsistency erodes trust faster than complexity.

3 — Cognitive Accessibility

Minimizes interpretation overhead. People navigate without needing to decode the system first.

4 — Distributed Civic Knowledge

Knowledge embedded in the system, not held by insiders. No prior familiarity required to engage.

5 — Equitable Access

Distributed without systematic disadvantage. Access can't be neutral when the system was built unequally.

6 — Meaningful Participation

Clear pathways to actual influence, not just consultation. Consultation without power is not participation.

7 — Institutional Transparency

Makes the relationship between actions and outcomes visible. You should know what you did and what came next.

From research to solution

Stage 3 — the transaction layer — is where administrative burden breaks trust and where CivicGuide intervenes. Principles 3 and 4 — Cognitive Accessibility and Distributed Civic Knowledge — became the direct specification for the AI layer.
