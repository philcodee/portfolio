The Design Brief

Goal

Improve how people understand, navigate, and trust public institutions — by helping them engage with civic resources without feeling intimidated or overwhelmed.

Problem Framing

Civic resources function less like supportive infrastructure and more like a series of isolated hurdles: forms to complete, meetings to discover, portals to decipher, deadlines to remember. The issue is not motivation — it is legibility, timing, and coordination.

A key distinction

Civic Resources — used on an individual or group level, in an infrastructural context, of a transactional or administrative nature. (e.g. NYC 311, DMV registration, benefit enrollment)

Civic Engagement — takes place on the community level, is social, participatory, and agentic in nature. (e.g. voting, city planning meetings, advocacy)

The research focuses on civic resources — the transactional layer — because that is where trust is built or destroyed in everyday life, long before anyone reaches the participatory stages.

Research Synthesis

Four themes emerged consistently across the research. They are not independent — they compound. Each one amplifies the others, and all of them fall hardest on people who already have the least capacity to absorb them.

Cognitive Load

Complexity isn't a usability problem here — it's an emotional one. Administrative burden gets experienced as an ordeal: the Gulfs of Evaluation and Execution loop continuously, with no guide and real stakes attached to every wrong turn.

Lack of Process Maps

People get stuck even when they're motivated. They know the problem — can't find housing, can't access healthcare — but can't see the steps between here and solved. A system that requires institutional knowledge to navigate is exclusionary by design, whether or not anyone intended it that way.

Stigma & Fear

For some communities, asking for help is itself a risk. Immigrant and undocumented residents face the sharpest version: seeking civic assistance can expose them to detention or deportation — meaning the help itself becomes the threat.

Time Poverty

Every barrier above lands hardest on the people with the least time to absorb it. Administrative burden isn't randomly distributed — it concentrates precisely on those who can least afford to carry it.

Trust has been falling for 50 years — and falling unevenly

Trust has declined almost continuously since 1972 — but the decline has not been equal, and the gap between groups has never closed.

8% — the lowest trust-in-government figure ever recorded for any group in this study: Black Americans, 2014.

User Mapping

Who carries the weight

Barriers cluster along four axes — age, region, cultural context, and income — and the overlap is where the experience becomes dehumanizing.

  • Young adults: procedural confusion; systems feel designed for someone else
  • Older adults: locked out of digital-first services
  • Rural: sparse infrastructure, long travel to service points
  • Minority communities: compounding distrust from historical exclusion
  • Immigrant communities: excluded by language, complexity, and fear
  • Low-income households: every barrier at once

Why it matters

Arnstein's framework maps where citizens actually sit in the decision-making structure — not where institutions claim they sit. Most transactional civic encounters (Stage 3) land in the Tokenism zone: citizens are informed, occasionally consulted, but rarely empowered.

Reducing administrative burden at Stage 3 is a prerequisite for climbing the ladder. You can't participate meaningfully in civic life if you can't complete your DMV registration.

Three problems, one root question

Every problem below traces back to the same question: how might we make civic resources feel like infrastructure that's on someone's side, instead of a series of hurdles to survive? Three angles on it stood out:

Influence — citizens are consulted, then ignored; pseudo-participation amplifies the feeling that the system is adversarial.

Burden — transactional experiences are tedious and opaque, and that cost lands hardest on people with the least time and information to spare.

Compounding — these barriers never arrive alone. For someone already time-scarce and wary of the system, they stop being inconveniences and become dehumanizing.

Seven principles, each closing one gap

Seven principles for designing civic products and services — each addresses a specific failure mode from the findings.

1 — Legibility of Civic Systems

Civic systems should be legible to ordinary residents without requiring specialized civic knowledge or institutional familiarity.

2 — Predictability and Consistency

Civic resources should behave consistently across time, institutions, and geographic contexts.

3 — Cognitive Accessibility

Civic systems should minimize unnecessary cognitive load and allow people to navigate them without extensive interpretation.

4 — Distributed Civic Knowledge

Civic knowledge should be embedded in environments and systems rather than requiring individuals to possess extensive prior knowledge before engaging.

5 — Equitable Access

Civic resources should be distributed in ways that do not systematically disadvantage particular communities. Access cannot be neutral when the system was built unequally.

6 — Meaningful Participation

Opportunities for civic engagement should have clear pathways to influence outcomes. Consultation without power is not participation.

7 — Institutional Transparency

Civic systems should make the relationship between actions and outcomes visible. People should be able to understand what they did, what happened as a result, and what comes next.

From research to solution

The administrative burden at Stage 3 and the opacity of government forms directly shaped CivicGuide's problem statement. Principles 3 and 4 — Cognitive Accessibility and Distributed Civic Knowledge — became the specification for the AI layer.
